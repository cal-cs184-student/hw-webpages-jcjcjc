Part 5: Shaders

A shader program is a small piece of code that runs on the GPU to determine how geometry is drawn to the screen. Because the GPU runs shaders in parallel across thousands of vertices and fragments at once, shaders allow us to achieve real-time rendering speeds that would be basically impossible on the CPU. A shader program is made up of two stages: a vertex shader and a fragment shader.The vertex shader runs once per vertex, applying the model and view-projection matrices to transform it into screen space, and writes out varyings like world-space position and normal that get interpolated across each triangle during rasterization. The fragment shader then runs once per fragment using those interpolated values along with uniforms like light position and camera position to compute a final color written to out_color. Together, the vertex shader sets up the geometry and per-vertex data, and the fragment shader decides what color each pixel should be.

The Blinn-Phong shading model approximates how light interacts with a surface by combining three components: ambient, diffuse, and specular. The ambient component is a constant term representing indirect light bouncing around the scene, which ensures surfaces facing away from the light are not completely black. The diffuse component models matte reflection that scatters equally in all directions, which is brightest when the normal points at the light and falls off with the cosine of the angle between them. The specular component models shiny highlights by computing the half-vector h between the view direction and light direction, then raising the dot product of the normal and h to a power p that controls the tightness of the highlight. Higher values of p produce smaller, sharper highlights, making the surface look glossier. Adding all three components gives a result that contains both the overall shape (through diffuse shading) and the material's shininess (through specular), while avoiding pitch-black shadows (through ambient).
