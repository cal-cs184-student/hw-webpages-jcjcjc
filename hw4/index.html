Overview

We implemented a real-time cloth simulator that models cloth as a grid of point masses connected by springs, along with a set of GLSL shaders for rendering it. In Parts 1 and 2, we built the grid with structural, shearing, and bending springs, then used Verlet integration and Provot correction to simulate its motion. In Parts 3 and 4, we handled collisions with external objects and with the cloth itself, using spatial hashing to make self-collisions efficient. In Part 5, we wrote GLSL shaders for diffuse, Blinn-Phong, texture, bump, displacement, and mirror shading to render the cloth with realistic lighting in real time.

The most interesting takeaway was seeing how each layer built on the previous ones to produce realistic cloth. It was also cool to see how parameters like ks and density produced very different results, and how bump and displacement mapping looked similar on smooth meshes but behaved very differently on coarse ones.

Part 2: Simulation via numerical integration

We experimented with some parameters of the simulation.

Spring constant

With a low ks, the cloth sags and stretches more since the springs are weaker. We see more rippling and wrinkling, and the cloth is generally more "bouncy". With a high ks, the cloth looks much stiffer and tighter. It holds its shape better and does not droop as much. The difference occurs and is most visible at the end of the simulation, when the low ks cloth sags mostly in middle of the cloth between the two pinned corners.

Density

Lower density makes the cloth feel tighter and more lightweight. Higher density causes more sagging and stretching, similar to what low ks looks like. The difference occurs and is most visible at the end of the simulation, when the high density cloth sags mostly in middle of the cloth between the two pinned corners.

Damping

With low damping, the cloth swings and flops around a lot before coming to rest, with lots of wrinkles and movement. It looks as though there is very little air resistance. With high damping, the cloth falls slowly and smoothly as one piece, barely flopping around at all. The difference occurs right when starting the simulation. Low damping causes the cloth to swing back and forth for a long time, while high damping barely lets it swing at all.

Part 3: Handling collisions with other objects

Sphere collisions: For each pointmass in the cloth, we check if it is inside the sphere by comparing its distance from the sphere's origin to the radius. If yes, then we compute the tangent point by extending the vector from the origin to the pointmass out to the sphere's surface. We then compute a correction vector from last_position to this tangent point and apply it scaled by some value for friction.

Plane collisions: For each pointmass in the cloth, we check if it has crossed the plane by comparing the sign of the dot product of (position - point) and (last_position - point) with the plane normal. If the signs are not the same, it crossed. We find the tangent point by projecting the current position onto the plane. The correction vector from last_position to this offset point (also scaled for friction) is applied.

With a low ks, the cloth drapes loosely over the sphere, closely mirroring the spherical shape, almost hugging the sphere. With the default ks = 5000, the cloth sits on top, slightly more rigidly. With a high ks, the cloth is very stiff and juts out over sphere rather than draping down.

Part 4: Handling self-collisions

To avoid the O(n²) cost of checking every pair of point masses, we implemented spatial hashing. At each simulation step we call build_spatial_map, which loops through all point masses, hashes each position into a 3D box volume using hash_position, and inserts the point mass into the corresponding bucket of our hash map. For hash_position, we compute box dimensions w = 3 * width / num_width_points, h = 3 * height / num_height_points, and t = max(w, h), then truncate each coordinate of the position to its containing box using fmod, and combine the three truncated coordinates into a single key using a linear combination with distinct primes.

Once the map is built, self_collide looks up the bucket for a given point mass's hash and iterates through the candidates. For each candidate (skipping the point mass itself), we check if the distance is less than 2 * thickness, and if so, we compute a correction vector that would push the point mass to exactly 2 * thickness away from the candidate. We accumulate all such correction vectors, average them by the number of collisions, and divide by simulation_steps before applying to the point mass's position. In simulate, we call build_spatial_map followed by self_collide on every point mass each timestep, so that self-collisions are resolved using updated positions.

Part 5: Shaders

A shader program is a small piece of code that runs on the GPU to determine how geometry is drawn to the screen. Because the GPU runs shaders in parallel, shaders achieve real-time rendering speeds that would be impossible on the CPU. A shader program is made up of a vertex shader and a fragment shader. The vertex shader runs once per vertex, applying transformations to move it into screen space and writing out varyings like world-space position and normal that get interpolated across each triangle. The fragment shader then runs once per fragment, using those interpolated values along with uniforms like light position and camera position to compute the final pixel color. Together, the vertex shader sets up the geometry and the fragment shader decides what color each pixel should be.

The Blinn-Phong shading model approximates lighting by combining three components: ambient, diffuse, and specular. The ambient term is a constant that ensures surfaces facing away from the light are not completely black. The diffuse term models matte reflection and is brightest when the normal points at the light, falling off with the cosine of the angle between them. The specular term models shiny highlights by raising the dot product of the normal and the half-vector h (between view and light directions) to a power p, where higher p produces smaller, sharper highlights. Together, the three components capture an object's overall shape (diffuse), shininess (specular), and softly lit shadows (ambient).

Comparing these two approaches, bump mapping adds a bumpy, rough texture without really changing the shape of the object, while displacement mapping actually affects the shape of the object. The bump mapping only affects lighting, so the silhouette of the object stays smooth even though the surface looks bumpy, and the displacement mapping actually moves the vertices, so it produces bumpy silhouettes with detail.

On both coarseness values, the bump mapping results kind of look the same. However, for the displacement mapping, on a lower coarseness, the bumps look more chunky and big, and at 128, displacement mapping produces fine-grained bumps visible along the silhouette.
